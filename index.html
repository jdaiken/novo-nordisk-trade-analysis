Trade Disruption Analysis

Novo Nordisk and the New Era of Pharmaceutical Trade Disruption

John Aiken  ·  BUS 280

Section AIndustry & Company Overview

The global pharmaceutical industry is one of the most trade-sensitive sectors in the world economy, subject to complex regulatory approval, patent protections, and government pricing controls across every major market. Within this environment, the market for GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) receptor agonists has become the fastest growing industry and perhaps one of the most controversial segments of medications. GLP-1 medications were originally developed for type 2 diabetes treatment and have since gained notoriety that have revolutionized obesity treatment. Especially in countries such as the United States, this market had generated enormous commercial growth.

Novo Nordisk A/S, headquartered in Bagsværd, Denmark, is one of Europe's most valuable publicly traded companies by market capitalization. Novo Nordisk is also a global leader in diabetes and obesity care. According to its 2025 annual report, the company was founded in 1923, and built its foundation on insulin manufacturing, where it still holds roughly 33.7% of the global diabetes market by value (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). However, according to its most recent 10K filings and its annual report, its recent growth is almost entirely attributable to two GLP-1 drugs: Ozempic (injectable semaglutide for type 2 diabetes), which exceeded DKK 120 billion in global sales in 2024, and Wegovy (injectable semaglutide for obesity), which reached DKK 58 billion with 86% year-over-year growth in just its fourth year on the market (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). Novo Nordisk's most recent annual report indicated that total company sales reached DKK 290.4 billion (approximately $41 billion USD) in 2024. This upward shift in sales is up 25% from 2023 with Obesity Care growing 57% at constant exchange rates as part of its portfolio (Novo Nordisk, 2025b). The company employs over 75,000 people across 80 different countries and markets its products in approximately 170 countries (Novo Nordisk, 2025a).

The United States represents approximately 50% of Novo Nordisk's global revenue, making it by far the company's single most critical market (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). This analysis demonstrates that given the increased interest in drugs such as Wegovy, this concentration creates both extraordinary commercial opportunity and significant political risk for both the United States and Europe.

Section BTrade Disruption Analysis

As part of its ever-shifting trade policy, the second Trump administration deployed an unprecedented and inconsistent policy assault on foreign pharmaceutical manufacturers, this strategy seemed to include three distinct but interconnected trade barriers that included government-imposed restrictions (including tariffs) that increase the cost or difficulty of international commerce. The impact of this trade policy is still very much ongoing and seemingly lacking any cohesive contours, but it certainly has redefined the trade relationship between the United States and Europe.

First, the administration threatened 100% tariffs on certain imported pharmaceuticals, with a stated October 1, 2025, effective date (Tankersley, 2025). Since the overwhelming majority of Novo Nordisk's active pharmaceutical ingredient production is located in Denmark (Kalundborg, Hillerød, Gentofte) and France (Chartres), such tariffs would have functionally doubled the landed cost of its products in the United States (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). Second, the administration implemented a "Most Favored Nation" (MFN) pricing requirements, mandating that all domestic and international pharmaceutical companies charge U.S. government programs no more than the lowest price they offer to any other developed nation (The White House, 2025). This directly limited the industry's longstanding practice of charging significantly higher U.S. prices relative to price-regulated European markets and caused even more ambiguity in Novo Nordisk's effort to redefine its trade policy in the US. Furthermore, for Novo Nordisk, the 2024 Form 20-F already disclosed that U.S. net prices after rebates declined in 2024 due to payer pressure which is a natural force of the market. MFN requirements threatened to accelerate and formalize this erosion even faster which may have made it not necessarily profitable or prudent to export to the U.S. at its current volume (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). Third, Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. publicly criticized GLP-1 medications as enabling overconsumption rather than treating disease, creating regulatory uncertainty around coverage decisions, FDA approvals for new indications, and broader public perception. The broader concern from the U.S. side is changes to pharmaceutical policy in insurance coverage under the Centers for Medicaid and Medicare (CMS) could make obtaining GLP-1 medications devastating.

The industry-wide impact has been significant. American rival pharmaceutical company Eli Lilly (which manufactures its GLP-1 competitor Mounjaro) domestically gained the both political favor and cost advantages from policies targeting foreign manufacturers (Kimball, 2025). Simultaneously, compounding pharmacies exploited supply shortages to produce cheaper unauthorized versions of GLP-1 active ingredients, fragmenting the market and undermining Novo Nordisk's premium pricing position (Novo Nordisk, 2025a).

Section CChallenge Identification

Challenge 1: Tariff Exposure and Government Pricing Pressure

As stated above, with nearly half of global revenue sourced from the United States and nearly all production based in Europe, Novo Nordisk faced an unprecedented market-access crisis (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). A 100% tariff would have made its products economically uncompetitive overnight to the United States (Tankersley, 2025), while simultaneous MFN pricing requirements threatened to shrink government-program margins (for instance CMS coverage) by up to 82% (The White House, 2025). The company needs an immediate strategy to preserve U.S. market access without sacrificing its ability to fund its global capacity expansion already underway (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). Recall this expansion began prior to the second Trump Administration meaning any strategic development was interrupted by unforeseen events.

Challenge 2: Competitive Disruption from Eli Lilly and Compounding Pharmacies

Even setting aside government policy, Novo Nordisk faces intensifying structural competition with American companies gaining the upper hand. Eli Lilly's Mounjaro compete directly in the GLP-1 pharma space with clinical data showing comparable or superior outcomes in some trials. Additionally, compounding pharmacies began producing cheap unbranded generics during the previous shortage periods a threat enabled by Novo Nordisk's own supply chain constraints (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). Additionally RFK Jr.'s ideological opposition (definitely an unseen factor in strategic planning) to these medications adds a further layer of market risk by potentially suppressing overall demand regardless of which company supplies the drug.

Section EAnalysis of Specific Trade Issues

Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)

Novo Nordisk's $10 billion commitment to U.S. manufacturing facilities is a textbook case of foreign direct investment (FDI) where a company in one country establishes ownership of productive assets in another. Traditionally, Novo Nordisk's FDI followed an efficiency-seeking logic business model that included concentrating the medication's expensive production in Denmark where specialized biotech expertise, regulatory certainty, and a century of institutional knowledge create genuine advantages. The company's main production facility in Kalundborg Denmark alone houses the world's most advanced GLP-1 production infrastructure (Novo Nordisk, 2025a).

The Trump administration's tariff threats forced a strategic pivot toward market-seeking FDI investment motivated not by production efficiency but by the need to access and retain a specific market. The company's U.S. expansion to Clayton, North Carolina (expected operational 2029) and the Catalent fill-finish site acquisition ($11.7 billion, December 2024) together represent Novo Nordisk choosing market security over production efficiency (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). This illustrates a key principle that when political risk makes a market inaccessible, FDI becomes a defensive tool rather than a cost-optimization strategy. It remains to be seen if Novo Nordisk remains competitive in the U.S. or decides to pivot its efforts to more favorable trade climates.

Comparative Advantage

The concept of comparative advantage advanced by David Ricardo that nations and firms should specialize in activities where their relative productivity is highest explains why Novo Nordisk built its production base in Denmark. Denmark long known for its world class biotech workforce, strong intellectual property rights, and decades of accumulated manufacturing expertise gave the company a genuine comparative advantage in producing complex biologic drugs that only the U.S. and Germany possess. The semaglutide molecule used to produce GLP-1 pharmaceuticals requires advanced protein engineering that cannot be easily replicated elsewhere (Novo Nordisk, 2025b).

However, the Trump administration's 100% tariff threat represents a government-imposed distortion of comparative advantage that no company could have foreseen. This forced Novo Nordisk to adapt to trade policy in order to artificially redirect production toward less efficient domestic locations (i.e. North Carolina) regardless of where it can be done best (Tankersley, 2025). Novo Nordisk's response (accepting higher manufacturing costs in the U.S.) is a direct acknowledgment that political risk can override economic logic in the short term, even for a company with objectively superior production capabilities in its home country. The United States in contrast should consider whether this short-term pivot will in fact pay off for the market or end up costing American consumers more in the long run.

Non-Tariff Trade Barriers and Strategic Trade Policy

The MFN pricing requirement announced by the Trump administration illustrates how modern non-tariff trade barriers restrictions beyond simple import duties can be more damaging than tariffs alone. By mandating price parity between the U.S. and lower-cost markets, MFN requirements effectively export foreign price controls into the U.S. market without formally restricting trade volumes which is a central tenant to the trade theory covered in Chapter 6 and 7 of our text (Hill, Chapter 6 and 7). This is economically equivalent to a revenue tax on foreign pharmaceutical companies but without any real clear strategy on the part of the administration. Inevitably, the cost will be passed on to the American consumer as we've started to see with the administration's tariff policy.

The broader policy context reflected here is strategic trade policy where government intervention is deliberately structured to advantage domestic firms (Eli Lilly) at the expense of foreign ones (Novo Nordisk). By exempting U.S. manufacturers from tariff exposure while targeting European importers, the administration created artificial competitive advantages that no amount of corporate efficiency could fully offset (Kimball, 2025). This is precisely the scenario that free trade economists argue reduces global welfare production shifts not to where it is most efficient, but to where it is most politically protected. Most concerning this creates a similar posture to the Smoot Hawley tariff scheme covered in Chapter 7 and 8 of our text (Hill, Chapter 7, 8).

Section FTrade Strategy Recommendation

Novo Nordisk's best path forward is a three-phase integrated strategy grounded in the data disclosed in its 2024 Form 20-F and Annual Report (Novo Nordisk, 2025a; Novo Nordisk, 2025b). The company has already implemented portions of phase 1 and the results are yet to be seen but if the company wishes to remain within the United States it may consider the following options below.

Phase 1 — Immediate (0–12 months): Secure Market Access Through Political Accommodation

The company should finalize and deliver on the November 2025 agreement with the Trump administration to accept price reductions of up to 82% for government programs, commit to $10 billion in U.S. manufacturing, and secure Medicare/Medicaid coverage at negotiated rates (Kimball, 2025; The White House, 2025). While this definitely hurts profit margins, this is the right short-term solution despite having to capitulate. The company already disclosed in the Form 20-F that U.S. net prices were declining due to payer pressure regardless of administration policy (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). By accepting formalized concessions in exchange for tariff exemptions and regulatory predictability converts an uncontrollable headwind into a managed cost even if the administration's policy is incoherent. Lower prices also expand the addressable patient population and may even lend it to better domestic insurance coverage.

Phase 2 — Medium Term (1–3 years): Embed in the U.S. Economy

Accelerate the building of the Clayton, North Carolina facility expansion (target operational 2029) and build a U.S. centric supply chain by sourcing components and raw materials domestically where feasible (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). Every American supplier, worker, and community stakeholder created becomes a political constituency defending Novo Nordisk's market position against future policy reversals. The Catalent fill-finish acquisition already demonstrates this logic by acquiring existing U.S. infrastructure is faster and politically more visible than greenfield construction (Novo Nordisk, 2025a).

Phase 3 — Long Term (3–5 years): Innovate Beyond the 2032 Patent Cliff

Novo Nordisk's semaglutide compound patent expires in 2032 (Novo Nordisk, 2025a). With eight years remaining, Novo Nordisk must create successor products with independent patent protection. The Form 20-F pipeline discloses the key candidates: (1) Oral semaglutide (Wegovy pill), FDA-approved in late 2025 for January 2026 U.S. launch, oral formulations are structurally difficult for compounding pharmacies to replicate; (2) CagriSema, demonstrated 22.7% weight loss in patient. These long term programs that reframe GLP-1s as broad therapeutic platform drugs, directly countering RFK Jr.'s characterization of these as lifestyle medications (Novo Nordisk, 2025a; Novo Nordisk, 2025b).

Throughout all three phases, Novo Nordisk should retain European manufacturing capacity and develop secondary production in politically neutral markets (Ireland, Singapore) as contingency against future U.S. policy shifts. The Novo Nordisk Foundation's controlling 76% voting stake which currently prevents hostile acquisition. Additionally this provides the institutional patience required to execute a decade-long strategy of this complexity (Novo Nordisk, 2025a).
